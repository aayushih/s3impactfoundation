Expanding and renewing the temple and cultural heart of Govardhan Ecovillage in Palghar, as visitor numbers grew from about one lakh a year to more than eight lakh.

## The challenge

After the Covid years, the number of people visiting Govardhan Ecovillage grew sharply. Around 2022–23 the campus received about one lakh visitors a year. By 2024–25 that had risen to 8,11,071 day visitors, counted by the campus security cameras, with festivals bringing especially large crowds.

The Sri Sri Radha Vrindavan Behari temple is the cultural heart of the campus, where visitors come for worship, kirtan, japa (meditative chanting) and to learn about Vedic culture. To welcome everyone who now comes, it needed to be expanded and fully redeveloped, from its hall and floor to its roof and surroundings.

## What S3 Impact Foundation made possible

S3 Impact Foundation supported the expansion of the centre by funding it.

Step by step, the temple gained a carved wooden ceiling, a marble inlay floor, a lift lobby, a fountain and inlay work in the sanctum. The inlay on the parikrama (the walkway where visitors circle the shrine) and a wooden altar for Srila Prabhupada were finished in 2025–26. In April–June 2026 the kalash (the finial on top of the spire) was fixed, the Mangalore-tile roof was completed and a new sacred image (murti) of Srila Sanatana Goswami was finished. Facade panels were being installed to complete the work.

## Elderly Care Home at Govardhan Ecovillage

A home where senior devotees live with dignity, daily care and a full spiritual life, at Govardhan Ecovillage in Palghar.

Completed · Elder care · Wada, Palghar, Maharashtra · 2024–2026

## The challenge

Rural Wada has a growing elderly population but few services for older people. Many seniors live with arthritis, diabetes or heart conditions. Long journeys and limited mobility make it hard for them to reach a doctor, and there are few places offering preventive care or rehabilitation. Senior devotees who can no longer live on their own need a place where they are cared for in body, mind and spirit.

## What S3 Impact Foundation made possible

S3 Impact Foundation supported the building of the Elderly Care Home by funding it. It is a long-term residence for senior devotees, designed as a home away from home. Its 30 new rooms will also welcome senior devotees who have settled at Govardhan Ecovillage from overseas.

Built for safety. The home is wheelchair-accessible, with ramps, wide doorways and accessible bathrooms. Rooms have raised beds and chairs with rounded edges, anti-slip floors, grab bars, an intercom, and emergency buttons by the bed and in the bathroom that alert the reception desk. An ambulance is on call on site.

Care every day. Doctors visit regularly, including specialists in geriatric medicine and Ayurveda, and nurses are on hand around the clock. The medical wing has a consultation room, a nursing ward with oxygen, monitoring and emergency equipment, and a physiotherapy clinic. Physiotherapy and gait training help residents stay strong and steady on their feet, and weekly assessments catch health changes early.

A full and joyful life. Residents share breakfast, lunch and dinner in the dining hall like a family, and meals are taken to anyone who is unwell. The day begins with the 4:30 am morning programme and includes chair yoga, activities, an afternoon class and an evening programme. Residents make garlands, sort tulasi leaves for worship, sing bhajans every Sunday, visit the cowshed (goshala) and gardens, and celebrate birthdays together.

Trained, caring staff. Caregivers are trained in first aid for older people, emergency response, fall prevention, emotional support and respect for each resident’s faith and culture. Clear manuals and records guide everything from admissions to housekeeping.

## The difference it made

Around 30 senior residents cared for, with 30 rooms in the new home

24/7 nursing and regular doctor visits on site

A daily rhythm of worship, classes, yoga and shared meals

Weekly health checks and a yearly health examination for every resident

Senior devotees at Govardhan Ecovillage now age in a safe, caring home, close to the temple and community they have served.

## Govardhan Annakshetra: Free Meals for Palghar’s Villages

2.4 million free meals in 2025–26 for tribal villagers, schoolchildren, young trainees and hospital patients in and around Palghar.

## The challenge

Palghar district, about 100 km north of Mumbai, is home to a large tribal population, and many families live in poverty. Child undernutrition is widespread. In parts of the district, children eat mostly rice and dal, with no milk. Families migrate for work, and children miss school. Patients who travel to hospital from remote villages often have no money left for food.

## What S3 Impact Foundation made possible

Govardhan Annakshetra is a large community kitchen that cooks free meals every day. S3 Impact Foundation helped by sponsoring these meals.

The kitchen opened in December 2018 and can cook up to 10,000 meals at a time. Machines such as a chapati maker, which turns out 800 chapatis an hour, keep up with demand. A head cook, an Ayurveda doctor and a food-safety manager set a monthly menu, using local vegetables where possible.

In 2025 the kitchen ran six meal programmes: - all-day khichdi (a simple rice-and-lentil dish) for anyone visiting Govardhan Ecovillage - full meals on campus for schoolchildren, farmers, women, families and visitors - cooked meals in tribal villages across five talukas - school meals for 200 residential students a day - three meals a day for tribal youth training at Govardhan Skill Centre - meals for about 200 poor patients and carers a day at Bhaktivedanta Hospital

The programme also plans monthly grocery kits for 100 vulnerable families and meals at large festivals.

## The difference it made

2.4 million free meals served in 2025–26

About 710,000 meals for skill-centre trainees in 2025–26

About 274,000 school meals for 200 residential students in 2025–26

About 191,000 meals cooked for tribal villages in 2025–26

The programme reports that village meals have raised schoolchildren’s energy and cut skipped meals, and for many families they are the main meal of the day. Patients’ families say the hospital meals eased the cost and stress of treatment. Between April 2025 and March 2026, full meals served on the Govardhan Ecovillage campus rose by 7.9%, and meals served through rural outreach rose by 31.2%.

## Growing Food for Govardhan Ecovillage’s Kitchens

S3 Impact Foundation is fully sponsoring the work to turn the land at Govardhan Ecovillage into a vegetable farm so that its community kitchens can grow more of the food they serve.

In progress · Agriculture and food security · Palghar, Maharashtra · From January 2026

| At a glance |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| Location | Govardhan Ecovillage, Wada taluka, Palghar district, Maharashtra (sites at GEV Adi, GEV Madhya and Nabadwipa Ghat) |
| Timeline | Phase I January–June 2026; a second phase is planned to run into 2027 |

## The challenge

The Govardhan Ecovillage community kitchens operate at the ecovillage and in Thane, Chowpatty and Pune. Each kitchen runs independently, but they work together towards a shared goal of self-sufficiency, with plans to source their vegetables from the ecovillage’s own farms. Together they need about 700 tonnes of vegetables a year, and almost all of it has been bought on the open market, at an average of about ₹30 per kg (2025–26). The ecovillage sits in Palghar district, where poor soil and erratic rainfall make farming hard. The question was simple: could the ecovillage grow a large share of those vegetables on its own land?

## What S3 Impact Foundation made possible

S3 Impact Foundation is fully sponsoring a two-year plan to farm the ecovillage’s land for its kitchens. The team mapped 50.1 acres across three sites and divided them into six zones. Phase I, from January to June 2026, focused on the first zone: 12.3 acres growing 10 crops. The second phase is planned to bring in the remaining zones, about 37.7 acres and 29 crops. The two-year plan has a proposed project budget of ₹2.10 crore.

By June 2026 the team had identified the land, built infrastructure across 6.145 acres, installed drip irrigation, completed the first cultivation and begun harvesting. Every harvest is now recorded digitally and tracked on a monthly dashboard, so progress can be measured month by month.

## Progress so far

The first harvests are in. The next steps are to expand cultivation, complete fencing, add rainwater harvesting, a nursery, weather and soil sensors, a cold store and more polyhouses (greenhouse-style tunnels that protect crops).

The long-term aim is ambitious: the plan is for the farm to meet more than half of the kitchens’ vegetable needs, at a lower cost than buying from the market.

## Bringing Braj’s Sacred Ponds Back to Life: Priya Kund and Prem Sarovar

Natural filters, microbes and local “friends of the kund” have brought a sacred pond in Barsana back to health.

Completed · Water and environment · Barsana, Uttar Pradesh · From February 2023

| At a glance |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| Location | Priya Kund, Barsana, and Prem Sarovar, Mathura district, Uttar Pradesh |
| Timeline | Survey from May 2022; restoration of Priya Kund from February 2023; upkeep continuing in 2026 |
| Supported by | S3 Impact Foundation |
| Key figures | 16 water bodies surveyed; Prem Sarovar water clarity up from 58 cm to 66 cm by March 2026 |

## The challenge

The kunds of Braj are sacred ponds and tanks in Mathura district. People worship at them, bathe in them and depend on them. A survey that began in May 2022 checked 16 of these water bodies and found that about 70% held “C” grade water, which national pollution control guidelines class as unfit even for bathing.

At Priya Kund in Barsana, three open drains, detergent, offerings from devotees and loose soil were all feeding algae. The kund only stayed full because a borewell pumped water into it non-stop.

## What S3 Impact Foundation made possible

S3 Impact Foundation turned Priya Kund into a working model for restoring Braj’s ponds. The work began with a survey using drone mapping, water and sediment sampling, and digital contour mapping. UP Braj Tirtha Vikas Parishad approved the resulting project report and, in February 2023, gave permission to work on six water bodies.

At Priya Kund the team:

planted trenches around the kund to trap silt and monsoon runoff

built a natural soil filter that cleans wastewater from a nearby building, then uses it to water the trench plants

added a small filter bed for abhisheka water (water used to bathe the deity in temple worship) from the Priyeshwar Mahadev temple, which had flowed straight into the kund

applied microbial cultures in stages to break up algae

Local Brajavasis (residents of Braj) were employed as kund mitras, “friends of the kund”, to watch over it and talk with visitors. Local artists painted murals, and signs now list what visitors should and should not do.

## The difference it made

According to our project monitoring, Priya Kund’s water quality improved markedly within six to eight months, and the kund mitras have kept it steady since. By July 2026 Priya Kund was reported to be in excellent condition, with both soil filter systems working. The results drew the government’s interest in similar work across the area.

At Prem Sarovar, a filter tank, a solar aerator and wall paintings are in place, and upkeep continued through 2026.

## Bhakti Bhavan, Kolkata: Restoring an Acharya’s Home

S3 Impact Foundation sponsored the reconstruction of Bhakti Bhavan, the 1881 Kolkata home of Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakur, now reopened to devotees and visitors.

Completed · Heritage and culture · Kolkata, West Bengal · 2025–2026

| At a glance |  |
| :-: | :-: |

## The challenge

In 1881 Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakur (1838–1914), one of the foremost teachers (acharyas) of the Gaudiya Vaishnava tradition, bought a house in central Kolkata. After a house-warming ceremony in 1882 he named it Bhakti Bhavan, the “house of devotion”, and it became a centre of his writing and worship.

From a printing press on the ground floor, called Chaitanya Yantra, he published many of his works. In 1896 he sent his English book Sri Chaitanya Mahaprabhu: His Life and Precepts to McGill University and the Royal Asiatic Society, the first time these teachings reached the West. His son, Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Saraswati Thakur, grew up in this house, and the deity of Sri Kurma Dev, found during the 1881 renovation, is still worshipped on its altar.

Nearly 150 years on, the building needed extensive work to survive as a living place of worship and pilgrimage.

## What S3 Impact Foundation made possible

S3 Impact Foundation sponsored the reconstruction of Bhakti Bhavan. The house has been carefully renovated and restored to how it looked in Bhaktivinoda Thakur’s time. Photographs from before the work show worn walls, old doors and a small altar kept in a wooden cabinet. Photographs after the work show a freshly painted three-storey street front with white balconies, new signage giving the building’s name and year, and a restored interior with a staircase of exposed brick rising from a chequered marble floor.

## The difference it made

A 145-year-old heritage home restored to its original form

Reopened to devotees and pilgrims, with a grand inauguration

Daily worship continues on the same altar where Sri Kurma Dev has been worshipped since 1881

Bhakti Bhavan is once again open to devotees and visitors, who can now experience the home where a key chapter of Gaudiya Vaishnava history was written.

## Safe Drinking Water for Govardhan Ecovillage

A multi-stage purification plant treating 12,000 litres an hour now turns Govardhan Ecovillage’s borewell water into water that tests free of coliform bacteria.

Completed · Water and sanitation · Palghar, Maharashtra · 2026

| At a glance |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| Location | Govardhan Ecovillage, Galtare, Wada, Palghar, Maharashtra |
| Timeline | Delivery July 2026; commissioned 8 September 2026; final report September 2026 |
| Supported by | S3 Impact Foundation |
| Key figures | 12,000 litres an hour · coliform bacteria removed |

## The challenge

Govardhan Ecovillage welcomes visitors from nearby villages and from across India and the world, and its kitchens, guest houses, schools and residents all depend on the same water supply. The quality of that water, used for drinking, cooking and daily needs, directly decides the health and safety of everyone on campus. Water that is contaminated or poorly treated can spread infections and waterborne disease.

The campus draws its water from a borewell. When the raw water was tested in September 2026, it contained total coliform bacteria. India’s drinking-water standard (IS 10500:2012) requires these bacteria to be absent from any 100 ml sample, because their presence shows the water has been exposed to contamination. The raw borewell water was not safe to drink untreated.

## What S3 Impact Foundation made possible

S3 Impact Foundation supported the installation of a water treatment plant that cleans 12,000 litres an hour.

Water passes through a sand filter, an activated-carbon filter, bag and cartridge filters, three ultrafiltration membrane modules and an ultraviolet (UV) disinfection unit. The ultrafiltration membranes hold back bacteria and cysts, and UV light deals with any germs that remain. Two 15,000-litre tanks hold raw and treated water, and an automatic control panel runs the system.

Why this technology. Each stage tackles a different kind of contamination, so the water passes through several barriers before it reaches a tap: - Sand filtration traps silt, suspended dirt and larger particles. - Activated carbon absorbs chlorine residues, pesticide traces and compounds that cause bad colour, taste and smell. - Ultrafiltration membranes have pores as small as 0.01 microns, small enough to physically hold back bacteria, viruses, protozoa and cysts, while letting the water’s natural minerals pass through. Unlike reverse osmosis, it does not strip the water of minerals or waste large volumes of water. - UV light gives a final, chemical-free layer of protection, killing any remaining germs instantly without leaving by-products in the water.

The result is a system that needs very little chemical dosing, has low running costs, and runs largely on its own, with automatic backwashing, alarms and pressure monitoring keeping the output consistent day and night.

The treated water tested free of both total coliform and E. coli. Two dissolved minerals, hardness and alkalinity, sit above the acceptable limit but well within the permissible limit. They come from the borewell itself and barely changed, because this kind of plant is designed to remove germs and particles, not dissolved minerals. A trained team at Govardhan Ecovillage now runs the plant.
